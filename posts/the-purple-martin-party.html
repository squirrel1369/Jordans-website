Chaplain Chat • August

The Purple Martin Party

Last weekend, I found myself sitting in a parking lot between an H-E-B and a Wendy’s with hundreds of complete strangers.

We were there for the Purple Martin Party, an Austin-area summer tradition where people gather at dusk to watch thousands of purple martins return to their communal roost, kind of like the bats under South Congress Bridge, only birds. The birds spend their days eating insects and, as evening comes, begin gathering in the sky before settling together for the night. The roost changes from year to year, so humans simply follow wherever the birds decide to be. Which, if you know anything about birding, then finding exotic birds or weird behaving birds in a fast food parking lot really isn’t that shocking. However, this was and is objectively a strange thing to do.

Hundreds of birds become thousands, and suddenly the whole sky seemed to be moving. They swirled and twirled and gathered together in ways that looked and felt almost choreographed. Eventually they disappeared into the trees for the night.

I had so many questions for these birds.

Where are you going?
How do you know when to turn?
Who tells you all to do that?
How do you know where everyone else is?
What does the world look like from up there?

I wished I could just pluck one out of the sky and ask it. But, of course, I couldn't. I could only watch.

And I've been thinking about that this week because there is something familiar about that kind of witnessing that happens in our work.

So much of our work is sitting with something we cannot fully explain.

There is an extraordinary amount that we do know about dying. We understand the physiology. We recognize the changes that happen as the body begins to shut down. We understand the purpose of comfort medications. We know what changes in breathing, circulation, consciousness, and appetite can mean. We have evidence, experience, protocols, and clinical knowledge that help us care for people well.

There is a science to dying. And yet, there is also a mystery.

We can understand what is happening physiologically without being able to know exactly what it is like for the person experiencing it. We can recognize Cheyne-Stokes breathing without knowing what the world feels like from inside that breath. We can understand why a medication may ease pain or air hunger without being able to know precisely what relief feels like to that particular person. We can recognize changes in consciousness without knowing what memories, images, relationships, or experiences might be moving through someone's awareness.

We can know the trajectory without knowing every detail of the journey.

Our science allows us to recognize what is happening and respond skillfully. It keeps us from being frightened by every change and allows us to help families understand what they are seeing. But our willingness to acknowledge mystery keeps us humble.

We know a great deal about the process of dying. And there will always be something about the experience of dying that remains a mystery. Our work is not to choose between those two truths, but to hold them together. To know what we can know. And to remain present for what we cannot.

Sometimes, as clinicians, we may feel pressure to have an answer for everything. But perhaps part of excellent hospice care is knowing when we have an answer and knowing when all we can do is remain curious, attentive, and present.

I couldn't ask the birds why they turned when they did. I couldn't know what they knew. I could only sit among a few hundred other people and witness something that was happening right in front of us.

That is part of what we are asked to do in our work, too. We bring the science and everything we have learned about the dying body. And then we sit beside a person whose individual experience will always contain something we cannot fully measure or explain.
